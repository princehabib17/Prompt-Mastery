Great case studies put the result in the first sentence, then let each layer below answer the question the layer above raised.

Reviewers scan before they read. The best case studies are built so a 10-second glance, a 1-minute skim and a full read all tell the same story, just at different depths.

If you only read this box
  1. Lead with the outcome. Put the answer first and the backstory after it (the inverted pyramid).
  2. Write subheads that make sense read alone. Someone reading only the headings should still get the whole story.
  3. Captions make the argument. Each image gets one sentence that says what changed and why it matters.
  4. Every number needs a before, an after and a timeframe. "13th → 4th in 6 weeks" beats "big ranking gains."
  5. Show what you didn't ship. Hiring managers say rejected options show judgment.

The reveal order

Six layers, widest at the top

Each layer is a place where the reader can stop and still leave with the right idea. Readers move down only if the layer above earned it. The reader-intent labels are a working model, not measured reading times.

Glancelisting card

Card title = brand + person + scope

Before anyone opens the page, the listing card already says who, what and (ideally) the result. Storetasker's customers page does this in one line: "How Cubitts met Roberto for cart and collection page upgrades."

Glancetop of page

TL;DR + one headline number

Two or three sentences: the problem, what you did, what changed. Then one number with its baseline and timeframe. If a reader stops here, they still have the full story.

Skimfact strip

Role, team, timeline, scope

A short strip of facts. NN/g's survey of hiring managers names "your role, any constraints, project timeline" as things they explicitly want to see.

Skimheadings + visuals

Headings and captions that tell the story alone

Headings that make a claim ("The size chart was the real drop-off") plus before/after images with captions. Reading only these should retell the case study.

Readbody

Decisions, tradeoffs, rejected options

Why this approach and not the others. One idea per paragraph, and each paragraph starts with its point.

Studyappendix

Method and proof

Test length, sample size, which metric, and what else changed in that window. Collapsed or at the end, for the reader who wants to check your work.

The narrowing shape is intentional: less text and more commitment as you go down. Nothing important should live only in the bottom layers.

How to reveal information

Nine rules from research and real examples

These rules cover structure only: order, labels, density and proof. They say nothing about persuasive copy.

1 · Start with the conclusion Research

In NN/g's reading study, 79% of test users scanned any new page and only 16% read word by word. Their recommendation is the inverted pyramid, "starting with the conclusion." For a case study, the result and the change go at the top, and the chronology goes below.

✗ "In March, the team reached out about their store…"
✓ "Rebuilt the PDP around fit data; add-to-cart rate rose from X% to Y% over 8 weeks."

Source: NN/g, How Users Read on the Web

2 · Write headings that say something Research

NN/g asks for "meaningful sub-headings (not 'clever' ones)." Generic labels like "Challenge / Solution / Results" help people find their way around, but they carry no information. Keep the label small and make the heading itself a claim.

✗ The Challenge
✓ CHALLENGE — Mobile shoppers never reached the size chart

Source: NN/g, How Users Read on the Web

3 · One idea per paragraph, point first Research

Readers "will skip over any additional ideas if they are not caught by the first few words in the paragraph." Open each paragraph with its point, and use bulleted lists for work items.

Source: NN/g, How Users Read on the Web

4 · Numbers need a before, an after and a timeframe Observed

Storetasker's strongest story (First Day, SEO) lists results as Calming gummies: 96th → 5th, with a start, an end and "within weeks." Their Boll & Branch story ends with results like "predictable technical excellence," which can't be checked. CRO write-ups also recommend stating the page tested, test length and sample size.

✗ Conversion up 38%
✓ PDP conversion (mobile): 1.9% → 2.6% · 90 days · vs. same period last year

Sources: Storetasker blog (First Day; Boll & Branch); Build Grow Scale on CRO case studies

5 · Captions carry the argument Convention

NN/g's portfolio template lists "images with captions" as a core part. Skimmers look at images before body text, so a caption should state the change ("Moved size guide above the fold; fit questions to support dropped"), not just name the image ("Homepage v2").

Source: NN/g, 5 Steps to Creating a UX-Design Portfolio

6 · Show what didn't make it Research

From NN/g's survey of 204 hiring managers: "I'm curious to know what isn't in the design and why, just as much as I'd like to know why elements made it in." Include one rejected direction per case study, with the reason you dropped it.

Source: NN/g, 5 Steps to Creating a UX-Design Portfolio

7 · Make your role obvious Research

NN/g lists "your specific role in the project and how you collaborated with others" as a required part. On a marketplace like Storetasker this matters even more, because the client, the expert and sometimes an agency all share the credit.

Role: Sole designer · Worked with: in-house dev + founder · Handoff: Figma → Shopify theme sections

8 · Name the tension before the fix Observed

Storetasker's newer stories have a "Core Tension" section with bold lead-ins: Why full-time hiring didn't fit, Why junior developers wouldn't work, What was at risk. Each option that was ruled out gets one bolded sentence, so a skimmer can read just the bold text.

Source: Storetasker blog (Boll & Branch)

9 · Use the same layout every time Convention

NN/g recommends "a basic template that you'll follow so that all of your projects look cohesive." When every case study has the same layout, returning readers know where the number, the role and the visuals will be, and they can compare projects at a glance.

Reveal mistakes

Six ways case studies hide their own point

Result at the bottom

Most readers leave before they scroll that far. Whatever you most want remembered has to be in the top layer.

Process diary

"Week 1… Week 2…" shows effort, not decisions. Organize by decision instead of by date.

Number without a baseline

"+38%" of what, compared with what, and over how long? Without those, a reader can't check the claim.

Uncaptioned screenshots

A skimmer sees the image but can't tell what changed or why it matters.

Only the final screens

NN/g: "the final screenshots only tell part of the story." Show one piece of process that changed the direction.

Everything given equal weight

If every block looks the same, nothing stands out. Emphasize the headline number and the key before/after, and keep the rest quiet.

Before you publish

The skim test

Hide the body text and read only the title, TL;DR, headings, captions and numbers. If the story still holds up, the case study is ready.
